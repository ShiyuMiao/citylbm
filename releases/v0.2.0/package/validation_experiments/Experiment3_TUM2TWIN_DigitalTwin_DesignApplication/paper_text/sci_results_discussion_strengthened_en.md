The additional statistical robustness analysis strengthens the morphology interpretation without changing the evidentiary status of the study. Across 101 retained building components, the 20-50 m local-context band preserves a negative association between enclosure and pedestrian-height wind recovery. The combined enclosure score is negatively correlated with directional mean VR (Spearman rho = -0.302, bootstrap 95% CI [-0.483, -0.097]), while sector enclosure within 50 m (rho = -0.396, 95% CI [-0.574, -0.158]) and mean height (rho = -0.351, 95% CI [-0.532, -0.147]) show the same direction of association. These intervals indicate that the morphology-response pattern is not driven only by a few extreme components. Rather, within the simulated campus core, local enclosure consistently suppresses the limited wind-speed recovery that occurs away from immediate facade-adjacent cells.

The tertile analysis gives this relationship a design-readable scale. In the 20-50 m band, components in the high combined-enclosure group have a lower mean VR than those in the low-enclosure group by 0.0043 (bootstrap 95% CI [-0.0076, -0.0014]). A comparable reduction is observed for local built fraction within 30 m (delta = -0.0039, 95% CI [-0.0072, -0.0009]). These differences are small in absolute VR because the entire pedestrian layer is already highly sheltered, but they are meaningful for interpretation: the morphology parameters explain relative recovery within a low-speed field, not a transition from stagnant to well-ventilated conditions.

The cross-validated model comparison further supports the priority of contextual morphology over isolated object descriptors. For directional mean VR in the 20-50 m band, a context-only model using local built fraction and sector enclosure reaches a mean 5-fold cross-validated R2 of 0.325, whereas a model based only on footprint area, height-normalized form, compactness and elongation gives a negative mean cross-validated R2 (-0.130). The best local-context model reaches R2 = 0.383. These values should not be interpreted as a high-precision predictive model, but they are sufficient for the paper's design argument: in this real digital-twin campus block, local enclosure continuity and near-ground connectivity carry more explanatory signal for pedestrian-layer wind recovery than single-building size or plan-shape descriptors alone.

Taken together, the FluidX3D and statistical results refine the architectural conclusion in three ways. First, the campus core is dominated by a robust low-speed pedestrian layer, so morphology mainly explains where small recovery occurs inside a generally sheltered field. Second, the immediate 0-20 m facade band and the 20-50 m local-context band should be interpreted differently: the former is almost uniformly sheltered, whereas the latter reveals the influence of enclosure release and passage connectivity. Third, the digital-twin contribution lies not only in producing wind maps, but in linking visual model audit, CFD-ready collision preparation, directional robustness, morphology statistics and claim boundaries into a reproducible design-evidence chain.

The S1 ventilation-relief sensitivity test adds an intervention layer to this interpretation. S1 removes 66 collision cells along a least-removal east-west corridor and reruns the same FluidX3D dx=2 m, 8-direction, three-sample protocol as S0. The result is near-null rather than beneficial: at z=2 m, equal-weighted mean VR changes from 0.076338 in S0 to 0.076124 in S1, and the VR<0.2 stagnation ratio changes from 0.933547 to 0.933780. Newly opened S1 cells remain entirely low-speed. This negative sensitivity result suggests that the stagnation field is not controlled by one local blockage; positive design claims would require network-scale porosity alternatives rather than a single light corridor.

The claim strength remains deliberately bounded. The current evidence supports a FluidX3D-native digital-twin screening and design-sensitivity study with CityLBM-compatible geometry preparation. It does not establish field validation, wind-tunnel validation, formal Lawson/NEN/AIJ comfort compliance, pollutant dispersion, a successful design optimization, a measured 3DGS-to-collision transfer error, or a completed Rhino-Grasshopper/CityLBM end-to-end run.
